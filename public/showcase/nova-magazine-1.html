The Last Lighthouse Keeper of Vardo

Ingrid Solheim 18 min read · Mar 2, 2025

The road to the light ends where the tarmac gives up and the gravel begins, and past that there is only a footpath worn into the rock by a hundred winters of the same boots. Ingrid Solheim walked it on a Tuesday in February, with the wind coming flat off the Barents Sea and the temperature holding at minus nine. She had come to meet the last person in Norway who still climbs a tower every evening to light a flame.

His name is Arvid, and he has kept this light for thirty-one years — through the era of the diesel generator, the era of the electric filament, and now the era of the automated beacon that was installed, officially, in 2002, and which he has never once trusted to work on its own.

The sea does not forget a name, even when the map does. Ingrid Solheim, The Last Lighthouse Keeper of Vardo

He speaks about the sea the way other men speak about a difficult relative: with patience, with a certain formality, and with the clear understanding that it will outlast him and that this is not a tragedy. What worries him is not the water. What worries him is the dark. Every autumn the nights get longer here, and every autumn he finds himself climbing the stairs a little earlier, as if the light might do some good that the schedule has not yet accounted for.

The village below has emptied out over the same decades. Where there were once four families on the cliff road there is now one, and a summer house that opens for six weeks and is shut the rest of the year. The shop closed in 2011. The school closed in 1998. What remains, besides Arvid and his wife, is a fishing harbour that still runs, a museum in the old radio station, and a light that turns every twelve seconds whether or not anyone is standing at the window watching it.

Every night I climb the stairs, and every night I check that it is turning. Arvid, keepers’ log, November 2024

Solheim stays for four days. She is up for the evening climb twice, and both times she notes the same thing: the ritual has nothing to do with the machinery. He does not check the beacon because he expects it to fail. He checks it because thirty-one years of evenings have made the walk up the tower the shape of his day, and because a man who has been given one job in one place is not easily persuaded that the job is finished.

On her last morning the wind drops and the sea goes flat and silver, and the light on the cliff is the only thing moving for a hundred kilometres in any direction. She writes in her notebook that the tower looks smaller than it did on the first day.
